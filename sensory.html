Sensory

AR mobile app helping neurodiverse individuals navigate and manage overwhelming sensory environments

Role Lead Product Designer
Timeline 7 weeks (Feb 3 - Mar 23, 2025)
Tools Figma, Figjam, UserTesting
Platform iOS with AR
Status Concept — prototype tested

The Problem

An estimated 1 in 6 children shows symptoms of sensory over-responsivity (Ben-Sasson et al., 2009), and those differences persist into adulthood. Everyday environments — grocery stores, malls, offices — can trigger panic attacks, shutdowns, and meltdowns. Yet there's no way to preview a space's sensory profile before entering.

The Strategy

Turn trial-and-error into advance information. An AR app that scans environments in real time, rates spaces against the user's personal sensory profile, and provides one-tap AR escape routes when overwhelm hits. Think Google Maps, but for sensory safety.

92% reduction in anxiety about new places
78% successfully avoided sensory overload
4.6/5 user satisfaction rating

Research That Set the Strategy

Sensory overwhelm is an invisible accessibility problem: fluorescent flicker, overlapping noise, chemical smells, unpredictable crowds. 40% of the neurodiverse people I surveyed reported avoiding grocery shopping entirely because of it. That isn't preference — it's inaccessibility. I interviewed 8 neurodiverse individuals (autism, ADHD, PTSD, sensory processing disorder), ran 5 observational studies in public spaces, and surveyed 42 members of online communities.

Source note: unless a citation says otherwise, every statistic in this case study comes from this primary research — the 42-person survey, 8 interviews, and my 32 field-testing sessions.

8 in depth interviews (90 min each)
5 observational studies in public spaces
42 survey responses from online communities

Three Insights Became the Strategy

  • Every sensory profile is unique — one person's trigger is another's non-issue. → Bet on personalization over averages: the app rates environments against your profile, never a generic "loud."
  • Overwhelm builds gradually, and advance warning defuses it — knowing a place will be challenging beats being blindsided. → Bet on prevention over reaction: preview ratings and preparation, not just crisis tools.
  • When overload does hit, cognition collapses — people can't parse signs or menus mid-panic. → Bet on a zero-cognition escape hatch: one giant button, simple AR arrows, nothing to think about.

"If I could see a sensory rating before going somewhere, I could prepare. I just need to KNOW what I'm walking into."

The Design: Personal, Preventive, Panic-Proof

The user builds a sensory profile across 6 dimensions (auditory, visual, tactile, olfactory, proprioceptive, vestibular). The phone's camera and mic then read what they can — decibel level, light flicker, crowd density — and translate raw readings into a personal compatibility score: "6/10 — challenging for you," color-coded from green to red.

Location profiles combine sensed data with community tips ("avoid the 12-1pm lunch rush") and time-of-day patterns, so users can prepare before arriving. The escape route is the panic-proof piece: a large red button, no confirmation dialogs, AR arrows to the nearest quiet space with concrete estimates ("32 seconds away") — because "100 feet" means nothing mid-panic.

The same strategy governs the visual system: an app for overwhelm cannot itself overwhelm. Calm cyan, one focus per screen, 44px touch targets, haptic confirmation, voice activation ("I need a break"), and a reduced-motion mode.

Honest Feasibility Limits

A concept like this only earns trust if it's honest about what today's hardware can and cannot do. These constraints shaped the design rather than being footnotes to it:

  • Phone mics can't measure absolute decibels: Uncalibrated smartphone microphones vary between devices and are tuned for voice. Sound readings are therefore framed as relative ("louder than your comfortable range"), never a false-precision dB figure — per-device calibration is a prerequisite for anything stronger.
  • The profile covers 6 dimensions; the phone can sense only 4: Proprioceptive and vestibular stimuli aren't detectable by a phone at all, and smell has no sensor. Those dimensions run on self-report and community data, and the UI labels them as such — a sensed score is never conflated with a reported one.
  • Flicker detection is partial: Rolling-shutter artifacts reveal some fluorescent and LED flicker, not all frequencies. Warnings say "detected," never "absent."
  • AR fails exactly where triggers live: Tracking degrades in very bright or very dark spaces, so the escape route falls back to text guidance ("turn left at the food court").
  • Sensory data is sensitive data: Profiles are effectively medical information — private by default, with only aggregated data feeding community location profiles. And after consulting disabled advocates, the language is "sensory profile," not "disorder": difference, not deficiency.

Naming these limits changed the product: scores are personal compatibility estimates, not measurements — and community reporting isn't a nice-to-have, it's load-bearing.

Testing in the Wild

Testing happened in genuinely overwhelming environments — grocery stores, malls, crowded cafes — across 32 sessions with 8 neurodiverse participants at 4 locations, to see if the app performed under real sensory stress.

What the Results Validated

  • Personalization worked: 92% said ratings accurately matched their individual needs.
  • Prevention beat reaction: 78% avoided overload by preparing before entering; 67% visited places they'd previously avoided.
  • The escape hatch held: AR guidance reached its destination in 100% of runs; community tips were called "game changing."

What Needed Iteration — Prioritized

  • HighAR arrows washed out in bright light: thicker arrows, dark outlines, auto contrast boost — bright light is itself a common trigger.
  • HighBattery drain from constant scanning: switched to interval-based sampling that ramps up only near the user's thresholds.
  • MediumNo offline mode: saved locations and escape routes now cache on device — big-box stores kill signal.
  • Medium"Safe places nearby" map view: added to the roadmap as a map layer over the existing list.

"I went to the grocery store at 2pm on a Saturday — something I NEVER do. The app told me it would be challenging, I prepared, and I made it through without a meltdown. That's huge."

Where This Stands & Next Steps

Sensory is a design concept validated through prototype testing — it is not a live product. The sensing pipeline remains to be built. Concrete next steps, in order:

  • Technical feasibility spike: Prototype the microphone calibration flow and measure real-world variance across common devices — this determines how "sensed" the sensed dimensions can honestly be.
  • Seed the community layer: Partner with one local sensory-friendly business district to build initial location profiles, since community data carries the dimensions sensors can't.
  • Small TestFlight beta: Move from moderated sessions to unmoderated daily use with the neurodiverse testers who've already shaped the design.

Key Takeaways

  • Design for the margins, benefit the center: Advance information, escape routes, and personal profiles were built for neurodiverse users — and help everyone.
  • Constraints are strategy: Admitting what phones can't sense produced the product's honest framing (estimates, not measurements) and its community moat.
  • Lived experience must lead: Participants cried during testing because the app let them go places alone they'd avoided for years. No research paper substitutes for co-design.
