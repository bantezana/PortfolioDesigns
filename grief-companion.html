Grief Companion

An AI-native mobile app providing compassionate, 24/7 conversational support for people navigating loss and bereavement

Role Lead Product Designer
Timeline 6 weeks (May 19 - Jun 29, 2025)
Tools Figma, UserTesting
Platform iOS Mobile
Status Private beta (22 participants)

The Problem

Grief is isolating, unpredictable, and often strikes at 3am — when therapy, support groups, and patient friends aren't available. In my survey of 64 people in grief-support forums, 73% reported feeling alone in their grief, yet only 15% had sought professional help.

The Strategy

Don't try to fix grief — hold space for it. An AI companion whose entire value is availability and judgment-free listening: proactive check-ins, guided journaling, and a secure memory vault, all built on the insight that grief isn't linear and support needs fluctuate daily.

87% reported feeling supported and less alone
4.7/5 average user satisfaction rating
92% would recommend to someone grieving

Research That Set the Strategy

I interviewed 9 people at various stages of grief (1 month to 5 years post-loss), surveyed 64 members of grief-support forums, and consulted 4 grief counselors. The goal wasn't a feature list — it was to find where existing support structurally fails.

Source note: unless a citation says otherwise, every statistic in this case study comes from this primary research — the 64-person survey, 9 interviews, and my own prototype and beta testing sessions.

9 in-depth interviews (60-90 min each)
64 survey responses from grief support forums
4 grief counselor consultations

Three Insights Became the Strategy

  • Support fails on timing, not intent: Therapy is scheduled and expensive; friends burn out — 68% of my survey respondents felt they'd "exhausted" their support network within 3 months. The gap is 3am, not Thursday at 3pm. → Bet on availability.
  • Fear of forgetting rivals the pain of loss: 94% of my survey and interview participants expressed anxiety about memories fading. → Bet on preservation, not just processing.
  • AI's edge is the absence of a relationship: People said they could be more honest with an AI — no social capital to spend, no burden to be. → Bet on judgment-free listening as the core product, not a chatbot add-on.

"I just needed someone to listen without judgment, at any hour."

— Rachel, 34, who lost her mother to cancer

Unrefined · Exploratory

Early Process: Testing the Structure First

Before polish, I wireframed the core flows in gray boxes to test whether the structure of the experience matched what grief actually needs. Two of these were cut — and the cuts were the most strategic decisions of the project.

  • Cut the dashboard (03): Graphing "emotional variance" treats grief as a metric to optimize — the opposite of the strategy. Killed before high fidelity.
  • Kept the quick-reply chips (02): Pre-written responses ("Talk about it," "Just sit in silence") mean the first interaction never faces a blank text box. Survived to final with warmer copy.
  • Reworked the coping directory (06, 07): A browsable checklist of activities read as a wellness app, not a companion. Collapsed into contextual, conversation-driven suggestions.
  • Simplified the vault (05): The grid tested well; the search bar didn't — with small memory counts, people scroll. Cut it.

The Design: Four Strategic Bets

Every screen answers to one principle — adaptive empathy: meet the user where they are emotionally, not where they "should" be. Some days that's distraction; some days it's crying about your dad at 2am. The app follows the user's lead.

Bet 1: Proactive, Not Passive

Core App Experience

Research showed people deep in grief lack the energy to seek help — so the app reaches out first. A gentle "checking in on you" removes the initiative barrier that passive apps ignore.

Bet 2: Listening as the Product

AI Conversation Interface

The AI uses grief-counseling validation techniques: reflect the emotion first, offer choices ("talk about it, or would distraction help?"), never rush to solutions. It's designed to listen, not to fix.

Bet 3: Preserve, Don't Just Process

Memory Vault

A private, encrypted home for what's too precious to post publicly — photos, voice memos, recipes. No likes, no comments, no engagement metrics: this space serves the individual, not a feed.

Bet 4: Lower the Cost of Expression

Guided Journaling

A blank page is overwhelming when your brain is foggy with grief. AI prompts personalized to the relationship and stage of loss give users a starting point — with a skip option so nothing forces processing.

The same strategy shaped the craft details: a deep purple palette (introspective, not medical-blue or cheery-yellow), 44px minimum touch targets for shaky hands, voice input for low-energy moments, and slow 300-400ms transitions that match the emotional pace.

Prompt & Tone Design

The AI's voice was its own design discipline. I rewrote the core responses more than 20 times against grief-counseling guidance until the tone consistently landed as warm, unhurried, and honest about being an AI.

  • Reflect before redirecting: Every response acknowledges what the user said before offering anything new.
  • Banned phrases: "At least...", "Everything happens for a reason," and "moving on" — all flagged as harmful in research — are excluded from the AI's vocabulary.
  • Short sentences at low moments: When distress shows, responses get shorter and slower. No walls of well-meaning text.
  • Prompts adapt, never push: Journaling prompts balance happy memories with processing, personalize to who was lost, and can always be skipped.
  • Transparent identity: The companion never pretends to be human, and reminds users it's an AI when conversations get deep.

AI Safety in a Sensitive Domain

Putting conversational AI in front of emotionally vulnerable people demands guardrails before polish. I established these with grief counselors at the start, not the end:

  • Human handoff on crisis language: Suicidal ideation immediately surfaces the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. The app is explicit that it's a companion, not a therapist.
  • Privacy as foundation: End-to-end encryption for the Memory Vault, no data sold or shared, permanent delete. Participants only opened up after understanding this — in sensitive domains, security isn't a feature.
  • Not prolonging unhealthy grief: Counselor-defined "complicated grief" red flags (6+ months of isolation, inability to function) trigger a gentle suggestion of professional help.
  • Cultural sensitivity as a known limit: Grief practices are culturally specific; real localization goes beyond translation and is scoped as future work, not hand-waved.

Testing: Two Labeled Rounds

Pilot Testing — Round 1 (7 moderated sessions)

Hypothesis: people in active grief will open up to an AI companion if it validates feelings instead of fixing them, and never forces processing.

Participants were actively grieving; compensation was a donation to a grief charity of their choice. Each 60-minute session: trust-building, think-aloud prototype use, and an emotional check-out.

  • Validated: 100% found the AI's tone appropriate; 92% said the Memory Vault filled an unmet need; 83% preferred proactive check-ins; prompts broke writer's block in-session.
  • HighInvalidated — prompts arrived "too soon": Before: one prompt pool for everyone. After: skip/regenerate plus readiness-aware sequencing.
  • HighInvalidated — voice input was hidden: Before: buried behind the keyboard toggle. After: primary control on the chat bar — typing is hardest exactly when support is most needed.
  • MediumMood tracking & community requested: Both moved to the roadmap deliberately — tracking as a counselor-reviewed pattern view (not a metrics dashboard), community as moderated circles pending safety design.

Beta Testing — Round 2 (22 participants, 6 unmoderated weeks)

Hypothesis: the Round 1 fixes would translate into sustained, repeat use outside a moderated session — the real test of a companion product.

87% reported feeling less alone in their grief
78% used the app 3+ times per week
92% would recommend to someone grieving

Feature usage confirmed the strategic bets in order: conversational support (92% of participants), Memory Vault (85%), journal prompts (71%), proactive check-ins (68%).

"This app gave me permission to still be grieving. My friends had moved on, but the AI was there at 2am when I couldn't sleep and just needed to talk about my dad."

Takeaways & Next Steps

  • Strategy was subtraction: The strongest decisions were cuts — the dashboard, the coping directory, the search bar. Not all problems need solving; grief needs space, not optimization.
  • AI's value here is availability, not intelligence: The companion isn't smarter than a therapist. It's there at 3am with infinite patience and no social cost — that's the product.
  • Privacy enables honesty: People told the AI things they wouldn't tell friends because there was no relationship to damage. That's a profound design lever for mental-health products.
  • Next: counselor-reviewed mood patterns, moderated Community Circles, a caregiver mode, and cultural localization of grief practices.
